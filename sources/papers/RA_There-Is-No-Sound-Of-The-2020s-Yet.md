
# There Is No Sound Of The 2020s. Yet.
[There Is No Sound Of The 2020s. Yet. · 特集記事 ⟋ RA](https://ja.ra.co/features/4505)

Part.1 [[RA_There-Is-No -Sound-Of-The-2020s.-Yet.]]
Part.2 [[RA_No-Music-on-a-Dead-Internet]]

How will this decade be remembered? Musically, it's unclear. In this special two-part report, Gabriel Szatan examines the forces fragmenting electronic culture today, and where we might go next.



Earlier this year, I spent an entire night drifting around a very prominent club stacked with very prominent DJs, and I felt nothing.  
  
On paper, the bill was strong: a mix of veterans and breakthroughs from different generations, including several outright stars in their field. But by some deadening alchemy, the sets smushed into a homogenous paste, largely indistinguishable in tempo and tone. At one point, you could make out a pinched expression on the headliner's face as she struggled to connect with the crowd. The main room shut hours ahead of schedule, a rare sight witnessed by just a dozen or so stragglers under cold, bright lights. I couldn't help but think, "Uh oh, the party might be over."  
  
If it was an outlier, it could be written off, but I can count multiple times over the last year that I've felt some version of that same restlessness, or that it bounced around conversation as a hot topic. Perhaps you've felt it too. No matter the soundtrack, the feeling echoed: this is good, not great. The lightning-in-a-bottle effect that animates club culture seems to be in hibernation and it's not just hampering nights out, either. As of late, electronic releases seem to exist on this same endless plateau. We have more music to enjoy than ever, but what's increasingly nagging at me is that this overfamiliarity is actually the problem.  
  
Every facet of our digital and physical existence nudges us towards more of the same: streaming platforms optimised for continuity, algorithms trained on our biases, expensive nights out that block many from rolling the dice on a different experience, festivals dictated by market logic. Accordingly, the space for novel sounds to lay down roots is being crowded out.  
  
The problem of disposability is hammering entertainment across the board, but isn't music meant to inspire a deeper connection? Although it's ugly to admit, faced with a wall of unopened promos, glossed-over notifications and albums that seemingly [evaporate](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/infinite-scroll/why-albums-drop-and-movies-launch) after first contact, music has become prone to throw-it-on-the-pile-itis.  
  
Electronic culture finds itself in a strange double condition: hyper-fragmented in theory, yet consensus-driven in practice. What used to feel like solid land has given way to an archipelago of disparate scenes with little awareness of their neighbours and, broadly, only slight modifications to preexisting sounds. That suits the already keyed in, but it makes following the underground hard work and locks out younger listeners who bring the fresh perspectives culture is crying out for.  
  
The only real connective thread is that everyone is pummelled by the same financial and technological headwinds, ones that not only trap creativity but strip away the energy needed to stay curious. The radicalism underpinning sharp art and the patient listening to appreciate it usually sit downstream of beneficial conditions, and as those conditions— cheap rent, functioning welfare, personal liberties, self-sufficient independent nightlife— are being withdrawn simultaneously, the precarity becomes more acute.  
  
Static progression and low conversion rates for young listeners are bad enough. But so far, the 2020s have failed to produce a new sound that has reached anything like a mass audience and that's another existential problem in the making.

**The alternative is circling a familiar neighbourhood over and over, repackaging iterations of the same sensation**

I mean this with total sincerity: where's the next _Remain in Light_ coming from? The next _Homogenic_, _Untrue_, _Black Secret Technology_, _Oil of Every Pearl’s Un-Insides_, _Double Cup_? A standard-setter with the potency to reach mainstream ears without sacrificing nuance, that receives the support to power it there. Will we really reach 2030 without an electronic classic for the decade to call its own?  
  
It's not that we're falling out of love with clubbing or music. [Plenty](https://ra.co/features/4501) of _Resident Advisor_'s [reporting](https://ra.co/features/4444) in the last [two years](https://ra.co/features/4472) suggests we're finding ways to deepen our relationship to sound without necessarily taking the plunge on _new_ sounds. Usually, when you sense one movement is on the wane, you have some hunch about what might follow, based on tremors in the field. What alarms me now is the absence of that tremor.  
  
The issue isn't just that we've bargained off the future due to an overreliance on the past, or that late capitalism is throttling progress, as the great accelerationist theorists Mark Fisher and Franco Berardi have previously argued (though both are true to an extent). The doomer take is that we've simply run out of new ideas, but I don't buy that. Petabytes of data suggest that something fresh _is_ out there. Whether we have the ability to reach it, an on-ramp to integrate it or the headroom to even care are the thornier questions.  
  
What follows is an attempt to chew through four interlinked ideas: the importance of movements that coalesce and reach saturation within club culture; the widening gulf between the industry and the tastes of a new generation; how risk-taking might dig us out of a rut even as the economic walls are closing in; and, in a companion feature, _[No Music on a Dead Internet](https://ra.co/features/4504)_, the twin threats that a crumbling digital archive and resistance to generative tech might pose. They can be read linearly, or tackled as modular components as you wish.  
  
Think of this as less a report from the heart of the dance floor, and more one from behind the screen—which, for better or worse, is where the culture increasingly circulates. Some of the conclusions and proposals will inevitably wind up off the mark, and I'm not so bold as to presume this piece will move the needle for the season ahead. I, like you, will enjoy a summer's worth of parties and tunes already cooling on the mastering desk. But it's time to start thinking in half-decade stretches, not season-to-season.  
  
It's worth asking ourselves what exactly the 2020s will be remembered for musically. If we're invested in breaking new ground, time's running out to get that in motion. The alternative is circling a familiar neighbourhood over and over, repackaging iterations of the same sensation until the culture ages out.  
  
So what exactly is gumming up the gears of progress? And how to fix it?

## Chapter I The Age of Adjacency

Do the 2020s lack a definitive sound? Ask different people and they'll tell you there have been many _sounds_. And they'd be right.  
  
There's always incredible, future-facing club music to be found. Take the freetek tribalism Spekki Webu and Woody92 are marshalling on Mirror Zone and Omen Wapta. Or the Latin rhythms that have permeated DJs' crates to an unprecedented level. You can look to London parties like experimental vortex [Trauma Unit](https://ra.co/promoters/149133) or [HouSupa](https://www.instagram.com/housupaofficial/)'s blend of Afrohouse, funky and amapiano, [Hypnus](https://ra.co/labels/10798), [Peak Oil](https://ra.co/labels/8518), [TraTraTrax](https://ra.co/labels/18226), [Incienso](https://ra.co/labels/15164)—hell, _everything_ coming out of Brazil.  
  
Yet while the Cambrian explosion of creativity in the '90s was never going to be replicated, each passing decade of the 21st century feels as though less is breaking through. The problem isn't that electronic music has stopped producing quality. It's an issue of quantity. There's more than the culture can reasonably metabolise, and, at a time when many pockets of the underground are still rummaging through the crates of the past, this bottlenecks the potential for new movements to cut through. Without anything to act as both a galvanising force and, occasionally, an antagonist to challenge, a lot of cultural motion effectively peters out.  
  
It's not just an issue of originality, but time, money and access: keeping up with what's fresh usually requires the wherewithal to go deep on the unfamiliar, as well as the advantage of living and existing freely in key cities that are home to leading nightlife. It's a vicious cycle that worsens the more that music drifts into passive consumption, encouraging lean-back listeners and suppressing artist compensation.  
  
And yet, when the scenes that do break are too distant or context-reliant to appeal to all but the most invested, listeners will be pushed into the arms of basal, familiar pleasures. That's a clear thread tying together the 2020s to date.

**Artists from outside the culture keep blandly finding themselves on dance floors, while serving up retreads of genres that have been around for decades**

It's worth pointing out that dance music of a more mainstream flavour has spent the decade doing… pretty well!  
  
Thanks to the likes of [Chris Stussy](http://ra.co/exchange/833), Solid Grooves and the FUSE set, groovy house has surged in popularity and shows no signs of flaming out. Under the aegis of [Interplanetary Criminal](https://ra.co/exchange/828), Sammy Virji and ec2a, speed garage and bassline have also dominated much of the decade, mounting an [unlikely comeback](https://ra.co/features/3726) that pushed far beyond northern England and the Midlands. For many ravers under 30, the 2020s will be defined by labels like ATW and Up The Stuss, the closely guarded USB serving as their equivalent of the dubplate. The snag is that, while massive, these are outgrowths of lived-in sounds, a new lick of paint on a classic motor.  
  
At ticket-selling level, what we might call the Teletech generation remains a commanding presence. The hard dance-techno-schranz axis is clearly one of the lightning rods of the 2020s, a natural route for younger listeners who favour all things fast. Yet, given its divisive sound and the reckoning over [allegations](https://ra.co/news/84624) that engulfed the scene, it's hard to believe it will be remembered as _the_ defining mode of the decade.  
  
The rising wave of Zoomer electronics might. The bright, noisy meld of sounds—including EDM, emo, rap, glitch and, in the hands of [femtanyl](https://www.instagram.com/femtanylll/), even breakcore—already lives in the big tent by default. By dint of its connection to associated scenes, you can draw all kinds of links from it: the likes of Bladee, Danny Brown and Skrillex are all only one step away and tap in regularly. (Skrillex's [CONTRA](https://ra.co/promoters/184735) even launched last month as a tactical move to house many of these modes under a single roof.)  
  
It may seem too terminally online to cut through, with a penchant for corrosion that makes most older listeners slam the ejector button, yet scope the font sizes on many of this year's major festival bills, and it's Bassvictim, [underscores](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DRMuAkjkc8B/), fakemink and 2hollis going up, while a lot of 2017 favourites shrink to the bottom line. Pay heed.  
  
Then there's Fred again.., as strong a star as dance music has produced this century. With unrivalled [pulling power](https://ra.co/news/84858) and the kind of gaiety that comes with financial privilege, his radiant anticynicism has proved irresistible for Gen Z, in turn supercharging the popularity of your Overmonos and Caribous in the States. Still, one star a decade does not make.

Pivot to the underground and it's a little different. The top beats in 2024 were easy to identify in real time: "Flight FM," _Brat_, raveslop in retreat and pop edits collapsing faster than an Ibiza Final Boss shitcoin. Try and put your finger on how last year felt and it's trickier. In a year absent of crackle, or a clear song of the summer, crowning any record #1 seemed strange, so we switched our best [records of 2025](https://ra.co/features/4350) to function more as a cartography of various electronic slipstreams. Five of the revitalised sounds you're liable to hear back in the hands of DJs at, say, Houghton right now—tech house, dub techno, dubstep, minimal and prog—lack a clear aesthetic identity and, you could argue, lack risk too.  
  
To date, the 2020s in club and electronic music has been more defined by current affairs than releases itself. A stop-start six years rocked by enormous, schismatic events will tend to do that. On record, the responses of a largely nonverbal music to a pandemic, racial protests and an ethnostate's genocide were always bound to be thin. The scene has principally expended energy on reorganising itself to support those affected through direct action and solidarity funds; rare attempts at commentary, such as Terre Thaemlitz's [searing treatise on Zionism](https://soundcloud.com/resident-advisor/ra1000-terre-thaemlitz-aka-dj-sprinkles), can only travel so far.  
  
But if lockdown foisted a universal event upon everyone, there was one defining spike in the DJ booth in its aftermath. Roll your eyes, but I've come to see that lively uncorking of energy and chaotic tempo-ramping as a parallel to punk's three-chord immediacy and electroclash's eye-popping aesthetics. The music and fashion burned hot, nabbed liberally from the past, rattled purists and was generally impossible to ignore.  
  
That was four years ago now. Where select dance floors find themselves now is post-punk, and not just because of Moin [adding](https://m-o-i-n.bandcamp.com/track/im-really-flagging-or-i-trusted-u-feat-ben-vince) rackety percussion over reversed Reece bass. We've arguably arrived at an intermediary zone of coiled tension, all dislocated and dubbed out, reflecting the general anxiety out there. These pitstops can often be fruitful, a level playing field for new ideas to stake their claim—so long as they cross-pollinate.  
  
At a time when the landscape is thick with scenes roughly adjacent to one another in scale and style, this makes prioritising who to back complex. As the pipeline that ferries emergent talent from early slots to peak time slows, you can see the impact in key bellwethers for electronic music: 2026 lineups heaves with talent, yet the presence of artists who released their most crowd-animating work 20 or 25 years ago pops out more than usual.  
  
Part of the problem could be that we lack zippy genre tags for a lot of what's going on. It's odd that no one has coined a name yet for the kind of skippy, pastel garage house with hints of trance that's been everywhere for the past five years (Four Tet-core?). Similarly, the luminous strands braiding [CCL](https://ra.co/features/4303)'s subglow, [Kia](https://ra.co/podcast/973)'s Animalia, Special Guest DJ's 3XL and acts like [Polygonia](https://ra.co/podcast/955) and [Konduku](https://ra.co/podcast/967) elude capture. When [covering](https://www.untitled909.com/untangling-the-web-of-the-french-low-end-psychedelia-scene/) murmurs in the European underground, journalist and publicist Chanel Kadir hit upon "low-end psychedelia." It's a winner that you can really extrapolate to mean the mid-2020s set who've taken over the headsier end of festivals worldwide.  
  
Yet attempts to clearly articulate what they do best has led me to a smudge of nearly-not-quite descriptors resembling one of those [cortex-scrambling images](https://x.com/melip0ne/status/1120503955526750208): illbient-shaded, bass-heavy, wormholing, rhythmically slippery, somehow downtempo in gait even when the BPM runs much higher. It's not, in short, easy to package up. Plenty of musicians over time have tactically avoided pigeonholing for good reason, but when a sound isn't immediately legible, it's unlikely to stimulate people outside its own nerve centres.

**"I'm sure there's a scene that's peaking right now that we know nothing about. It'll be young people doing something amazing, and we'll hate every bit of it because we're old" – DJ Harvey**

At a technical level, some of today's most compelling electronic music exists in adjacency too.  
  
In a sense, electronic fingerprints are everywhere. Despite being the lingua franca of 21st-century production, I fear it's increasingly operating as scaffolding rather than the driving force. Be it hallucinatory textures wedded to discrete songwriting (ML Buch's _Suntub_), log drums undergirding R&B (Tyla's "Water"), 8-bit distortion roughing up teen angst (Jane Remover's _Frailty_) or an entire solar system of avant-garde sketches (Dean Blunt's World Music), 2020s music already belongs to hybridists.  
  
Here lies the dilemma: is electronic music's role in these moments compelling enough to convert people into good?  
  
None quite hit with the force and future shock of prior waves that pushed electronic music into the speakers of millions and made active fans of the form. Today, the innovation that blooms from club communities on the progressive margins or in the Global South could hammer dance music's revelatory qualities, yet generally stays at that level. You're unlikely to catch Tame Impala or Beyoncé folding tambor drums or singeli tempos into their music any time soon.  
  
And so, mid and large tier artists from outside the culture rally around the blank slates of disco, house, techno and rave, blandly finding themselves on dance floors while serving up retreads of genres that have been kicking around for decades. This is how dance and electronic remains a perennial reference point, optically ubiquitous while materially unconvincing, shorn of the context and urgency that makes the sound command attention. In reality, it's little more than a revolving carousel heading nowhere fast with diminishing returns.

## Chapter II The Value of Movements

Electronic music _should_ still generate movements. Unlike so much conspicuous consumption in modern media, it has site-specific purpose and theoretical futurism hardcoded into its DNA. Many of its sub-genres are too arcane to see mainstream co-option, yet strong enough to sustain decades of influence. There's also, plainly, an infrastructure of nerds propping the thing up.  
  
As we watch multiple adjacent sounds get [jammed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aI0euMFAWF8) in the door and tap our feet for the next rupture, it's useful to recall how new movements have historically formed and sprung to prominence. Ideally, each release should function as a stop on the road rather than a self-contained event, boosting the likelihood of assimilation for those who want to take the plunge. When enough pressure builds around an idea or aesthetic that it starts organising reality around itself, you have a breakout. Then, once a sound erupts and crosses the threshold, it helps perpetuate its own longevity by multiplying the potential entry points for new fans.  
  
Do genres and scenes inherently seek recognition and canonisation? Is perception the only path to fulfillment? Of course not. ([Plenty](https://ra.co/features/2269) try hard to outrun it.)  
  
Still, no one complains that we understand the contours of techno or hardcore _too well_. Even if your first contact is the steel drums and vocal cut-ups of future garage pinging around Madison Square Garden for [30 nights](https://www.instagram.com/p/DT0uUlviOax/), or a [snatch](https://ra.co/news/84329) of IDM on YouTube Shorts, it all counts. There's a clear reason why ravers with no relationship whatsoever to one another all still idolise classic Midwest house: they made contact with it while it was hot and broad enough to reach the cheap seats. That's the reciprocal sweet spot for music-makers and listeners alike.

**With three years to go, a late burst of creativity to define the decade is still in range**

The '90s has been well turned over by now, so what can we learn from the previous decade? At least three substantial, unique movements were generated in the 2010s: EDM, hyperpop and retromania, all large and notable enough that dozens of variants fold into, and spin off, from them. You could also point to drill, or the overlap between Afro house, Afro tech and amapiano; same too deconstructed club, whose gleeful determination to ram through raw, uncategorisable music from the margins still has dance floors in a spiritual headlock.  
  
EDM is the least respected of the three, which is probably axiomatic to anyone reading _RA_. The genre supersized dance music as far as it could go, yet it would be absurd to deny its impact. As well as earning power players and [catfishers](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/business-44857336) stacks, its maximalism unlocked millions of fans who might've otherwise lain dormant, especially in America and Asia. So much creative friction was generated that entire communities pitched themselves in opposition to it, and when was the last time _that_ happened? Much of it was gaudy. Plenty of it was shit. A movement doesn't need to be tasteful to be real.  
  
For all the ways hyperpop and [_&&&&&_-style](https://p-a-n.bandcamp.com/album/-) hyperreal electronics riffed it still landed as a clean break, repositioning electronic pop around textures and transhumanist desires largely absent from the contemporary lexicon. Through faceshopping and digital interfacing, its first-wave icons updated the electronic principle of 'becoming one with the machine,' extending it far beyond rigging up a 303 and 707. More importantly, it was the first electronic movement in decades to be spearheaded by queer and femme producers. Its influence has rippled for so long now that the 2020s may actually be typified as an extended hyperpop era.  
  
The slipperiest of the three is retromania, less a hard sound than a change in mindset as nostalgia ran rampant, and expanded archives pulled whole histories of sound into circulation. In the 2010s, a flood of reissues met a desire to gain a multipolar understanding of music history. Backed by a core of specialist outlets in their prime, swathes of rare music transferred from obsessives and ethnomusicologists into the hands of casual listeners. The cult of the selector peaked, and from there, dance floors and listening bars fell under the sway of Patrick Cowley, Midori Takada, Saâda Bonaire, William Onyeabor, New Age, [bubblegum](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=10dKeg73nMo), minimal wave and others who had never played a starring role.  
  
In parallel, producers also spent great energy tearing at the threads of our half-remembered cultural fabric, a far cry from today, where edits are decried as cheap imitation and multiple scenes, including drum & bass, are [threatened](https://www.facebook.com/dbridgemusic/posts/thoughts-on-a-growing-theme-in-dnb/1481393280061903/) by "homage [as] the new product," per dBridge. At the time, this retrofixation produced some of the more intriguing work to break out of electronic music, from The Caretaker and Oneohtrix Point Never to vaporwave and synthwave.  
  
They might not have been as ubiquitous as house or jungle, but all three spawned active communities, infiltrated the live music and rave circuit at a deep level, informed what we see today as seminal releases, and continue to ricochet. They were, in a word, definitive.

Now, though, the fracture of universal experience means we may never see a coherent sound of the decade again—in this or any decade. Without that, culture risks becoming even more aleatory than usual.  
  
In the age of the infinite scroll, that's come to mean a flood of clips that rush down our feed at random. Be it Dek bass thundering out of [mobile sound systems](https://ra.co/features/4361) or the odd So Inagawa tune with [ten million plays](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=59BIX16RSkU), there's always a nugget of gold that satiates the algorithm's desires and goes viral for good reason. But for each one that makes it, there are millions that don't.  
  
Some emerging scenes are optimising in response. There's a refreshing lack of pretension in multiple Asian styles that, in [the words](https://officemagazine.net/club-temple-omega-sapien-buddhism-and-brain-rot) of producer Omega Sapien, are only a step away from "short-form-focused brain rot music." Whether they're actually enjoyable to listen to is up for debate. Besides, outside of the most [ardently committed](https://pitchfork.com/tags/rabbit-holed/) reporters, 15-second snatches of culture feel too scattershot or unserious for the industry to really map, let alone champion.  
  
Ultimately, the transience of all this worries me. The system we operate in today is not only disjointed and corporatised; worse, it's allowing creativity to escape into the air without capture. Who will document it? Are there going to be compilations and compendiums as there were before? We all hope that the most exciting tunes are not only out there, but should find their way to us in the end. The many ways music is upped, dumped and forgotten about these days, like digital debris swilling around the ocean, makes that feel unlikely.  
  
The irony here is that the difference between your, my and anyone's feed means any examples I throw out are liable to elicit slow blinks. Instead, the easiest way to illustrate the current state of play is with a heater. Do you know the biggest dance hit of the year so far? It's "[Just the Way You Are](https://open.spotify.com/track/1LepWHpwtahzEQL0vNjpEt)," first released by Italian house group Milky in 2002, then propelled back to ubiquity with a viral Mall Grab edit-turned-reissue. The tune has spent four straight months in the UK Top 20, and, yet, I found myself astonished by how many people around me—dance music professionals, no less—are completely unaware this was going on.  
  
Little wonder there was no unifying hit of summer 2025; the fracture is real. It feels like entertainment, just as reality, has tilted on its axis over the past two years.

**The 2020s ear values releases that feel catalytic instead of rigorous, which suggests we're pining for the opposite of stasis**

The positive spin is that this isn't the first time [the last rites](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2004/nov/03/popandrock) have been read for quality electronic music's ability to reach wider audiences. Consider the culture of 20-odd years ago. Dance charts were littered with frivolous tunes cribbing hooks from Hall & Oates and Eddie Murphy, while superclubs's cachet had evaporated. Big-hitters like Aphex Twin were on sidequests, filter disco was in a low pass, minimal was K-holing and criminalisation had wiped out the American rave network.  
  
And yet, in grubby London basements with only a few dozen attending, one of the most transformative genres of the century was emerging from the molten ash of UK garage. Thousands of miles away in the States, final touches were being placed on the blueprints for a [stage show](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B38CY-4Rd6s) that would permanently alter the scale of live electronic music, while huge tranches of club music were being exchanged through hard drive swaps and web uploads. You wouldn't know it on New Year's Eve 2005, but only a few years later, dubstep and blog house would be inescapable on dance floors the world over.  
  
With three years of the 2020s to go, a decisive late-decade rush is still in range. Rewind the clock fully 50 years, and that's exactly what happened: _Trans-Europe Express, Low, Music for 18 Musicians, Oxygène, Ambient 1: Music for Airports, Suicide, King Tubbys Meets Rockers Uptown_, "I Feel Love," "Flash Light" and "Kiss Me Again" all arrived in a delirious 19-month burst between 1976 and 1978. Some of them took years to bed in, but collectively, they made up foundational scripts for ambient, industrial, dub, disco, synth-funk, no wave, minimalism and the arpeggiated euphoria to which this whole thing of ours is indebted. No clairvoyant could have seen that coming.  
  
Afforded curiosity, effective broadcast mechanisms and the right social conditions, movements can surge anew. It's never too late—but it might require a spirit of risk-taking to get there.

## Chapter III A World of 4s and 9s

Walking around festivals over the past 12 months, my mind has flashed to a foodie trend that _GQ_ critic Alan Richman [once christened](https://www.gq.com/story/alan-richman-egotarian-cuisine) "egotarian cuisine." What hit the table in high-end joints was technically magnificent, rich in process and context, yet at odds with what the customer truly wanted. The street eats championed by the likes of Anthony Bourdain and Jonathan Gold claimed victory over Michelin-bait in the end. A version of that sensation appeared to be haunting dance floors, and if the opening weekends of summer 2026 are anything to go by, we're in for a second serving.  
  
There's good music, sometimes extremely good music, and no shortage of skill, yet a nagging lack of intent. Crowds, taxed by the relentlessness of life, show up to switch their brains off and vibe. To pretend the disconnect is exclusive to the [Keinemusik crowd](https://ra.co/features/4469) or too many phones on dance floors is a cop-out. Those around me who also enjoyed themselves expressed similar opinions: at times, the scene felt as if it was on beta-blockers.  
  
This is where my hope that we might yet stumble into a thrilling new movement hits a logical flaw: music that fails to activate the pleasure receptors or that feels misplaced with their environment often results in crowd drift. One of the most radical sets I caught last summer, [33EMYBW](https://ra.co/dj/33emybw) at Draaimolen, uncoincidentally happened to be one of the lowest-attended. And by all accounts, 2025's most script-deviating new headliner, [¥ØU$UK€ ¥UK1MAT$U](https://ra.co/features/4447), has undergone more than a few faceplants on tour.  
  
Meanwhile, four of the big vibey five identified earlier (prog, tech house, dub techno and minimal) don't necessarily lend themselves to soaring highs or shock value in the hands of DJs. It's a Möbius strip of a problem that's closer to algo-age flattening than many realise: conditions make it unappealing for a DJ to play far-out, creating crowds who become less accustomed to fearlessness, which then suppresses DJs' risk-taking, which leads to further homogeneous tastes, and so on.  
  
But I _want_ the rough and the sweet, the good and the bad, the cringe and the divine. Don't you? Increasingly, I'm certain I'd rather live in a world of 4s and 9s than endless 7s.

**"People were going out constantly because they were so enthusiastic about the culture. Economic conditions are now fucked, and that's going to affect the way we interact with culture" - Batu**

The obvious rejoinder is that risk sounds lovely when you're not the one paying for it. The financial backing required to get any kind of foothold in the arts has never been higher, and so to gamble on an unprecedented pivot, or one that doesn't align with what your brand demands, is a tall order. You need a locked-in fanbase, secondary income stream or enough professional insulation to weather one release cycle as a miss. As a result, it's no surprise that some artists lean towards the middle, even against their better instincts.  
  
The simple act of releasing music has also never extracted more from people. Countless independent labels, musicians and outlets have found themselves shackled to a content rat race they didn't set out to participate in and are ill-equipped for. That, too, is likely harming the amount of genuinely original music we might be exposed to, downed by how photogenic or charismatic the person promoting it is.  
  
It's bad enough that some musicians choose to pump out generic variations on a well-worn formula. Techno still dominates the circuit, but can you put your finger on the last techno tune to break big _and_ blow minds? When the motivational markers to sustain an artistic career become so skewed that there no longer appears to be any difference in corporate aspiration between [John Summit](https://ra.co/reviews/36348) the Ernst & Young accountant and John Summit the headline DJ, it's not hard to conclude we might be cooked.  
  
This decade's double-squeeze of brutal financial conditions and flattening technology is doing more than just chipping away at artistry. It's also inhibiting audience taste. One of the more sobering conversations I've had on the _RA Exchange_ in the past year was with [Batu](https://ra.co/exchange/817). One passage in particular really resonated:  
  
"A massive thing when we're talking about young people engaging with art and culture is ticket pricing," he said. "When I moved to Bristol, there was this idea about people going out constantly because they were so enthusiastic about the culture, absorbing everything like a sponge."  
  
"That can give you vitality you carry for many years to come, and that's just not [financially] possible any more. We talk about this in a vacuum of club music, but the general economic conditions in the UK and everywhere are really fucked, and that in short is going to affect the way people interact with culture."  
  
Seasoned clubbers rightfully reminisce over a more carefree time, but the affordability factor gets overlooked. When tickets are £7 or $10, it's affordable to flop it. Your preferences can be shaped by hopeless duds as much as slam dunks, but when the cost of a night out can easily tick into three figures, the same algo-age issue rears its head and you default towards what you already know.  
  
Developing art true to your vision takes time, and if you have to work two or three jobs just to make rent, the sunk cost of all that you can't dedicate to your craft, or music discovery, becomes insurmountable. When life demands more without delivering equal benefits, it not only locks out those without reserves of time and money, but also saps creativity and the energy needed to elevate artists with something new to say. Essentially, as the world gets more expensive and progression stalls, we get sucked into cultural [stagflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagflation).

If not risk, then the remedy might be boldness. Time and again, what lingers are the moments we come into contact with music that possesses some alien force. When undertaking a postmortem of our [2000-2025 lists](https://ra.co/features/4480), the votes reflected this: prizing gutsiness and a lack of precision, rather than well-made records that refined an existing sound, beautiful as they might be. You could argue that such exercises are slanted by default, but if the amount of froth over insufficient quantities of [DMT electronica](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q04ILDXe3QE) or _Boomkat_-friendly [steel and flesh](https://odbpowell.bandcamp.com/album/the-ongoing-significance-of-steel-flesh-diag001) conveyed anything, it's that canonisation matters more than some would like to admit.  
  
At this stage, the 2020s ear probably privileges releases that feel socially catalytic over those that feel formally rigorous, which suggests we're pining for the opposite of stasis.  
  
Narrative is a powerful driver, especially so in a fractured world. Some of the most memorable club moments of the century (_Glass Swords_, "Fuck the Pain Away", "Anti War Dub", any number of swaggering [grime tapes](https://soundcloud.com/getdarker/slimzee-w-wiley-dizzee-rascal-gods-gift-dirty-doogz-dogz-rinse-fm-24112002)) are still propelled to this day on the tension between the before-and-after. Think too of J Dilla's unquantised swing; SOPHIE's textural ick; Ricardo Villalobos's capacity for esoteric brass recordings and maddeningly elliptical mixing, or lately, sets by Nono Gigsta and Kiernan Laveaux that push multi-genre mixing to the edge of possibility. They cut through _because_ of their weirdness, not in spite of.  
  
More recently, Los Thuthanaka made a [record](https://ra.co/reviews/36262) so hyperlocal and unconventional in execution that I can't imagine anyone hearing it and thinking, "I'm going to set out to make this.' But what I would hope they say is: "In spite of the likelihood of failure, I _am_ going to make art this radical."  
  
If these are the revelatory moments that stick, we need to better engender and materially reward that spirit. (Or, as a more vengeful motivation, if you're appalled by the drabness of the competition, what better time to jump into the fire and make something truly off-kilter.) Traditionally, it's young people who provide the voltage. Here's the rub: is electronic music actually hearing enough from them?

## Chapter IV Electronic Audiences Could Do with a Completely New Set of Inputs

In '90s Germany, a new word entered the political sphere in _Reformstau_. This was the collective understanding that change was necessary, yet owing to a combination of consensus logic, vetoes and fear of rocking the boat, reforms stayed in gridlock. The result was a political and economic class accused of coasting while conditions changed faster than they could keep pace.  
  
My feeling is that we're in a _Reformstau_ of our own. Most are well aware things are rocky. What's the solution? Whether you take a radical view, as Berlin cultural theorist Xin did last year with a stirring [call](https://wasted.reluctant.promo/read.html) to shuffle club culture into a care home, or a more modest outlook, it feels evident that electronic music's oversupply and option paralysis is causing damage. We cover the potential outcomes of various archive, AI and production topics at length [here](https://ra.co/features/4504), but there's another issue at play, too: a generational disconnect split across multiple levels.  
  
Gen Z catch undue heat. Yet every signal shows that the generation who were denied a conventional induction into clubbing mid-pandemic have developed differently, and we can surely do a better job of getting on their wavelength. Think of this set, roughly 25 and down, as Young Zoomers, rather than the ones soon turning 30, who are closer to Millennials and already decently embedded in the system.  
  
How to shrink the divide? And what could we adopt from them to freshen up stale air in the room?  
  
Forget easy marks like matcha raves and set aside the hangup that crunching culture down into digestible chunks is unedifying. Though their entry points diverge, with alcohol-anchored physical spaces struggling as we know, their conditioning to engage with electronic music in a less deferential way could be liberating for a culture that ties itself in knots over formalities. But that'll only happen once distribution reorients to meet them where they actually live, rather than trying to strong-arm them into a clunky, trad environment.  
  
Perhaps more than any one sound, the largest change of the 2010s was in visual language. At a DJ level, phones became begrudgingly acceptable, to the extent that even high-end stage production began to deprioritise LED walls in favour of schmoozers ringing the booth, Boiler Room-style. Our feeds became vertical video players pumping out drop clips whether we liked it or not.  
  
That visual language has morphed again in the 2020s, into hyperbolic booth videos, always-on Twitch streamers and post-_Cum Town_ edgelords with podcast kits. That swing is tricky: for starters, any mano- or fratty vibes tends to be a hard red line for a counterculture rooted in progressive, queer and Black spaces. These are also zones antithetical to the patient listening, and quest for transcendence, that electronic music relies on. No one will perfect the crispness of their sound design with one eye fixed on a scrolling chat or by clip-farming reactions to sick drops.  
  
What's trickier still is a widening social gulf. Beyond values, a club landscape still dominated by Gen X and Millennials voices can often magnify the worst of the former's pedantry, the latter's earnestness and the obsessive streaks of both. Spats, self-righteousness, a 700-page entry manual required to onboard: it doesn't feel very welcoming nor, if we're being honest, particularly cool.

A parallel problem is the insularity of the industry; really, the call is coming from inside the house.  
  
Those who drive coverage or bookings often use a narrow and unreflective set of parameters, failing to pick up signals and overlooking huge communities as a result. It's less a functioning system than a hall of mirrors. The culture exists in a house of mirrors. Observed from the side of streaming, the amount of well-backed names in the contemporary underground who have near-zero organic pickup with listeners is startling.  
  
There's an evident flaw when agents, publicists, writers, radio hosts and promoters all largely drink from the same well of taste. It's an issue which dogs all media, including _RA_. That Substack's [favourite crusader](https://firstfloor.substack.com/) against corporate patronage in the arts served at _Red Bull Music Academy_ for years certainly throws up questions, but such was the state of centralised journalism; I was there too. And when _RBMA_, _FACT_, _Tiny Mix Tapes_ and other outlets faded out, the talent pool didn't fade with them. Critics with differing beats but roughly similar outlooks and connections kept their foothold in the industry, inspiring confidence that a wide-enough net has been cast to capture how borderless music in 2026 _really_ moves.  
  
At a time when the industry is in perpetual survival mode, throwing resources behind dispatches from the margins or doing the daily work to challenge your own listening habits, slides down the priority list. Still, it's little wonder a new generation fails see themselves reflected within a legacy media and walled-off rave circuit, which only worsens the two-way barrier.  
  
Electronic communities could reverse fossilisation by becoming accessible and rousing for onlookers from all possible angles. How much time expended on upholding heritage could be trained outward instead? How many producers benefit from this closed-off state of affairs? From Denver to Caracas, Surabaya to countless small towns with 22 year olds fiddling on software, those who could power the electronic music of the future risk slipping the net. In the long run, making inroads with them should demand greater urgency than satiating the tastes of a set bound to outgrow it.  
  
As data zips by faster than we can often document it, the transference we see as crucial is unlikely to take place in an orderly fashion we've been accustomed to. Electronic music has been caught oddly flat-footed by generational shifts, lapped by major genres like pop and rap, who are more agile, launch new stars into orbit regularly and replenish the listener base faster. By contrast, a didactic approach and stiffness to change has saddled electronic music with excess baggage.  
  
Still, changing preferences shouldn't be mistaken for the abandonment of specialism. It doesn't always show up in the avenues the industry and lived-in listeners recognise, but it's easy to spot. Off-grid parties are as massive as ever. The gradual _Needle Drop_-ification of to-camera critique has reshaped the field into one where creators like [marg.mp3](https://www.nts.live/shows/margeaux), [Tia Ho](https://www.instagram.com/tia_ho__/) or [RamonPang](https://ra.co/podcast/1053) continually make the niche scalable. More Young Zoomers are likely to find out about braindance or Love Spirals Downwards through them than by poring through old issues of _Faith_, and the profusion of information online means individual pools of knowledge still run deep.  
  
The commitment and ferocity of feeling is still one hundred percent there. You just have to work a little harder to unlock it—which makes the upcoming baton pass all the more crucial.

## Chapter V The Best Culture Is One That Makes a Convincing Case for Itself

A cycle may be ending, and that can be painful for many involved. Getting yourself psyched for a new one can be a lot, and we may have arrived at a point where knowledge and adherence to form leave people unable to conjure visions beyond the old frame. But intuition remains one of the rare qualities young people can bottle and act on before it gets talked out of them. There's a great deal worth taking from that.  
  
To that end, what constantly leaps out isn't only the adrenalised sound of earlier periods, but the _enthusiasm_. Where big moments in dance and electronic music land, so too follows a common thread: a scene you strove to be part of and a sound that drove you to obsession, and at club level, the absence of that is keenly felt. You can't beckon people to flood onto dance floors by rearranging the same old constituent parts.  
  
The present lack of churn is unusual. Time was, lineups would morph every few years, with cycles of death necessary for regrowth. The ratio of supply to demand may simply be out of whack, with too many mid-level DJs sticking it out and calcifying homogenisation. As paying the bills gets tougher, it seems unlikely many will volunteer to hang up their headphones and hand young people the power to reshape the industry from within. But the greater push may come down to unlearning resistance, too.  
  
There's obviously something to be said about retaining the purity and qualities of the sound you revere, a point so obvious it doesn't feel necessary to restate, but turning over the same stones for decades can make culture feel workmanlike and rote in the wrong ways. At its worst, the paternalistic imposition of the proper way to experience music acts as a limiter. Wagging the finger at EDM was briefly amusing for a moment in the '10s, but in short order, dance boomers posturing for likes in the comments of David Guetta's posts felt eye-scrapingly lame. Did anyone come away from those moments with a deeper sense of respect for dance music? Or thinking, '_This is cool_'?  
  
My mind goes to another document of reunified Germany, Rainald Goetz's 1998 novel, _Rave_. It's a wonderful snapshot of being swept up in a moment without precedent, but towards the book's end, the narrator remains unsure whether it had any meaning at all. As innocence unravels, Goetz stops to mull the fate of the collector-completists around him:  
  
“Olaf suffers, like most of the better DJs, from an ultra-refined fine-chiselled finical feel for musical elements. Its judgments turn ever more differentiated, ever more nervous, and ever truer—but may finally collapse into this collapsing of the truth where you wind up automatically with some monstrous marriage of vapidity and classicism, Goethe and Detroit, Chicago and the ancient Greeks. That doesn't add anything, doesn't go anywhere. It has nothing to do with the now."

The past is pockmarked by naysayers who mocked Throbbing Gristle as wreckers of civilisation, lobbed axes at Suicide, cried foul about the satanism of acid house, swiped at digital DJing as a low-bitrate bastardisation and dismissed PC Music as shallow art trash. You never want to be on the wrong side of history when it happens.  
  
"I'm sure somewhere there's a scene that's peaking right now that we know nothing about," [noted](https://www.ssense.com/en-gb/editorial/music/dj-harvey-interview-howlin) DJ Harvey, of all people, two years ago. "We won't even like it because we don't understand it. It'll be young people doing something we have no idea about, and we'll hate every bit of it because we're old, and it'll be amazing."  
  
It may be that the internet's totalising glare strips away the chance for anything to peak in seclusion ever again, a courtesy afforded to dozens of the scenes and sounds highlighted throughout this piece. Whether driven by a love of the chase or simply scrolling half-heartedly, we catalogue whatever we encounter in its infancy, then bounce to the next. It's an unsustainable approach, like water sloshing around above sediment that hasn't been properly tilled to nurture anything new.  
  
Perhaps the true sound of the 2020s will reveal itself in 2040, some Gauguin or af Klint thrust into vogue decades after production halts. It certainly sounds nice. But my hunch is we don't have time to wait that long.  
  
For a system struggling to amplify anything new, it feels incumbent on us all, especially those in the industry's swollen middle band, to shift our weight and think seriously about how to support the next breakthrough. Even at a time of collective exhaustion, to attempt no crossover, make no entreaties or reshuffle the pack if it comes to it, would be an error of judgment.  
  
The alternative path would be that electronic music loses resonance and drifts toward something like jazz in the late 20th century (and this is no knock on jazz). From the '60s onwards, jazz found itself successively pushed back from the mainstream, eclipsed by more youthful and enticing modes of entertainment. It fused with funk, went smooth, split into factions and was kept in suspended animation by formalists who claimed that an older vanguard's vision was the only way. Those who were locked in stayed locked in, but by the '90s, the crowd had largely aged out. It took decades for a renaissance to spark.  
  
In 1996, Francis Davis, acclaimed critic for _The Village Voice_ and others, [nailed it](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1996/07/like-young/376628/) when sounding the alarm about a looming generational cliff-edge:  
  
"In allowing itself to become so strongly identified with its past, jazz may be bargaining away its future… doesn't all this sanctimony about 'tradition' send a message to teenagers and college students that jazz offers absolutely nothing of interest to them? And isn't whatever the form of music that rings someone's bells at 18 or 19 likely to remain his or her music of choice for life?"  
  
Adjacency. A loss of bite. A crisis of accessibility. Resistance to modernise among the old guard, struggles to integrate a new generation, new modalities failing to cross over and break up stasis. The parallels are there if you care to see them.  
  
No one will share an identical view on what comes next, let alone what to do. But do you believe that _right now_ is the best you've ever had it? That if you started over at day one, you'd stick it out? That the electronic music and culture of the 2020s is vibrant and electric enough to go toe-to-toe with any other era? If not, there's two paths. You can either become a prisoner of the moment. Or you can laser in on what material change might look like, at any level, as a participator, facilitator, listener, believer. Then take it from there.
