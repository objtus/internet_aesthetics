# No Music on a Dead Internet

There are many issues hampering modern electronic music culture, judged at varying levels of urgency person to person: hypercommercialism, regressive politics, institutional failings, social brainrot, dwindling youth spaces, underfunding of the arts, and so on. All are valid. But there's one that colours more or less every interaction we have with culture, and is therefore trickier to probe without sparking a chain reaction of gloom. In 2026, the internet is dead. And we're trapped in it.  
  
For a scene anchored online, that's as high-stakes a problem as it gets. A fried internet harms both the past and the future. And in a moment of soul-searching about what's throttling progress, a space unfit to support the music that lives within it feels like a stark enough issue that we've split off this, a sister report to follow on from our main feature, _There Is No Sound of the 2020s. Yet._

[← Consider starting there first if you haven't already.](https://ra.co/features/4505)

We all know that a dependence on social media makes us sick. So rather than go over old ground, here we laser in on the compounding issues of archiving and AI. They might feel distant from the buzz of a dance floor in the hands of a good DJ, but they're no less crucial to the value, accuracy and spread of culture. No matter how overwhelming it may seem, complacency towards our crumbling digital environment threatens it all.  
  
The relationship new listeners have with electronic music is different than any generation before, so if you can influence behavioural patterns at the source, you stand a better chance of fomenting a lifelong love of it. The solution isn't as straightforward as claiming dance music needs to return to communal spaces, nor that young clubbers simply hit a rave and get inspired. It's romantic, but in 2026, it honestly feels as if we're past that.  
  
The answer lies in building stronger digital systems that can preserve culture, as well as tapping into the future with pragmatism, to avoid getting left behind as a new era dawns. This is how we propose to go about it.

## Chapter I The Time To Get Archiving Is Yesterday

Everything about the internet in 2026 is a mirage. Stats are inflated. [LLM](https://cloud.google.com/ai/llms) hallucinations have muscled out knowledge. That comment section argument you're reading is just two bots scrapping. The music you thought you liked was a [psyop](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/apr/29/geese-outcry-phoney-virality-music-fans-hype), actually.  
  
We find ourselves locked in the corporate maze of big apps and bombed-out webpages with no aftercare, a far cry from what the web was meant to deliver. Despite initially making more music more accessible, the irony of the present-day internet is that genuinely original or useful platforms are less likely to cut through than ever, even as a prospective userbase is glued to their phones. Accordingly, the motivation to create new music discovery tools, let alone conjure fresh ecosystems, has taken a hit.  
  
As detailed in Jace Clayton's seminal 2016 memoir, _Uproot_, it wasn't so long ago that the promise of the web opened new portals and powered a global gig network. For a music lover, the '00s blog-and-forum ecosystem was incalculably more beneficial than the one we groan through in the '20s: more character, broader specialism, less worry for engagement, no ring lights required. Hell, even early social media was decent by comparison; Facebook Events looks better in the rearview with every passing year.  
  
But it goes beyond exasperation with the phones in our hands. Collective memory is leaking out. We tend to consider the ephemerality of global electronic music, which often gestates through WhatsApp, memory cards or pirate markets, as priced in. But ever more micro-scenes are being lost to time without preservation—killer music that never finds a home in the present, nor survives for those looking for it down the line.  
  
Having wriggled free of analogue record-keeping, we've somehow wound up in a worse state than ten or 15 years ago. There are no guarantees that knowledge we've amassed is going to make it intact to those who will power the industry in the 2030s. You hear a lot of static about the lagging cultural awareness of Zoomers, but it's noisy enough to eclipse the real question: is there not an imperative to pattern up and build a stronger bridge from the past to the future?

Memory degradation poses a threat both explicit and subliminal. Take one most are familiar with: lost media. From an old fileshare link swallowed by a casino ad to cloud uploads with revoked access, through to a screenshot on a desktop as the lone proof of an untraceable document, we've all _not been able to find the damn thing_.  
  
We've traded physical objects like hard drives and magazines for a system that, while convenient, is also inherently fragile. In recent years, digital media has begun to resemble rows of condemned buildings, still standing yet prone to structural collapse under the weight of 404s or a swarm of ads. Even dweller's strong [library](https://dwellerforever.blog/library) of Black American and Afro-Palestinian literature, books and audiovisual ephemera isn't infallible. Try to access, say, the 2003 David Mancuso documentary _Maestro_, or _SPIN_'s 1998 feature on Detroit, you'll be met with a depressingly familiar line: "Sorry, but the page you're trying to view doesn't exist."  
  
Archiving does seem to be gaining more awareness in fits and spurts. You can see it in [WHOLENEW.WORLD](http://wholenew.world/) or the popularity of Marcus Barnes's [2010s Rave Archives](https://www.instagram.com/2010sravearchives/). Yet while the seismometer occasionally sends up readings that rock the feed—an _MTV News_ scare here, a MixesDB panic there—these cortisol spikes don't cohere into sustained action. "It's crazy," [CCL](https://notagspodcast.substack.com/p/03-ccl) wrote in 2023, "that an entire community, hundreds of mixes, music, content, comments can be gone in just a minute." Why is that? Shouldn't we be startled into action?  
  
The hidden cost of labour behind maintaining centralised archives is vast, and at a time when just about everyone is thrashed into a pulp by digital existence, it's fair to throw your hands up. But the presumption that the music we wish to access will always be at our fingertips, available to listen to and learn from, may end up being a tripwire in the coming years.  
  
Plenty of writers over time have sounded out the importance of proper documentation, from Ann Powers and Chris Zaldua to Emma Warren and GG Albuquerque. What feels extra urgent now is that the future of our very internet is being retrained on a partially-depleted, partially-paywalled log. In a moment of transference from Web2 into a strange AI-powered network, the issue of ahistorical preservation is worth snapping into focus. Models can be swayed with targeted action, but without that, false continuity will ripple in ways we can't control.

## Chapter II Lost Histories and Fragile Ecosystems

So much of what we know is already susceptible to human fault, but LLMs trained on an inaccurate ledger will only reproduce these errors at scale. Take "indie sleaze," one of the most prominent examples of a new term being retconned as the default name for an era. To be fair, the name works. But the fact that a tag blending electroclash, indie rock, blog house and hip-hop was coined and monetised by a Toronto-based influencer in 2021, who swiftly captialised on buzz to flip a series of namesake parties, raises a red flag.  
  
Should promo and prologue get jumbled this way? It wouldn't be the first time: the name hardstyle was originally trademarked by Dutch festival behemoth Q-dance in 2002 before sticking around. Yet it still leaves a strange taste. Gradually, the participants within what we used to call dance-punk or nu rave get faded out, [blanching](https://www.instagram.com/p/DWzNadJDbMg/?igsh=ZTR3ZzcwZXVmb2c1) a culture that was as much about nonconformist DJing, squat parties and a spirit of teenage amateurism as it was James Murphy or Alice Glass. As new hybrids of the 2020s are already getting called sleazepop, the lineage is liable to distort further. It won't be long until we arrive in a blurred future where [DJ Hell](https://ra.co/features/4493) and [The Dare](https://ra.co/reviews/36192) bum cigs in a hallucinated time loop.  
  
Archiving takes many forms, and you could feasibly call any capture of a sound a step in the right direction. As detailed in [the main piece](https://ja.ra.co/features/4505), the connections we can now draw between surges of parallel creativity in the late '70s or early '90s are proof of a strong archive at work, gaining coherence as a result of proper preservation.  
  
But in a splintered century, there's an obvious risk that comes when whole cultures nest in just one or two locations online. It's far easier to pull up examples of micro-scenes that are fiendishly hard to rummage through today (such as hardbass or instrumental cloud rap) than ones who enjoy extensive, perhaps even excessive, documentation (like Berlin techno). Often it comes down to locale or language. For artists who trusted platforms and then found themselves vaporised all the same, it's a tragic state of affairs.  
  
Today, MySpace brings to mind a calamitous 2015 server migration, proof that wipeout can just… happen. An estimated 50 million songs by 14 million artists were lost, accompanied by an exculpatory message suggesting people simply retain their back-up copies. (Helpful.) However, this wasn't their first blunder. In 2009, an enormous chunk of footwork history vanished off the face of the earth during MySpace's bottom-dollar deal to acquire [imeem](https://www.wired.com/2009/12/myspace-imeem-deal/), a social network, filesharing service and early digital storefront. One of the genres that we consider among the better disseminated of the 2010s suffered a heavy hit as a result of its carelessness.  
  
"Imeem acted as an incubator for footwork in the mid-2000s when the genre was evolving at a rapid rate," explained Tom Lea, of Local Action and _No Tags_, in a series of emails.. After News Corp, who owned MySpace at the time, rushed to asset-strip and absorb the smaller service, "an incredibly important archive at a key part in the genre was lost. As a result, the history of footwork is unfairly weighted towards the music that was signed by labels like Planet Mu and Hyperdub… it's a sad fact that certain artists have been written out of it as a result."  
  
Lea noted it's one of many sinkholes that have trapped "deeply important music" in the last 15 years: the demise of South African dance store [AFROdesiaMP3](https://web.archive.org/web/20121002225904/http://www.afrodesiamp3.com/catalog/index.php), which took with it all trace of thousands of records in 2012; the sampling bureaucracy and scattered _WorldStarHipHop_ loosies that limit appreciation of Jersey Club; even _Pitchfork_ sibling site _Altered Zones_, whose focus on the American DIY is now entirely M.I.A.  
  
It's ridiculous than deep-pocketed corporations might do a worse job at protecting and preserving music than niche labels pressing up 200 records at a time, or committed fans publishing zines and scanning in liner notes. We campaign to save the night, not the data. Shouldn't it be both?

**"Don't forget there are people who are willing to work to ensure that works of cultural value can be preserved and shared" – What.cd**

In 2026's state of atrophied archives and decaying UI, _fun_ outlets for music discovery and cataloguing are few and far between. If the enduring strength of NTS Radio proves anything beyond the impact of a human touch, it's the template set by their Infinite Mixtapes: a simple yet insanely effective way of nudging casual scrollers down the wormhole. More non-algorithmic distribution mechanism could be transformative in the years ahead. Are there options out there?  
  
One service that survived the early-'20s NFT crash, until [last week](https://ra.co/news/85295) at least, was _Nina Protocol_, a music hub woven around two blockchains, whose company culture was shaped by the stalking threat of digital wipeout. While the platform formed an excellent resource for new music, especially bubbling acts yet to show up on the radar of most DJs, Nina's editorial arm and sphere of influence tended to overshadow the size of its community: capping out around 40,000 users, it was undeniably one for the heads. Lamentably, so it stayed.  
  
A pair of niche services I'm fond of are [zig-zag](https://www.zig-zag.fm/) and [cosine.club](https://cosine.club/). zig-zag guides users across interlinked landmasses of sound, offering rolling extensions to listening binges while updating the cutesy feel of Y2K-era [genre maps](https://music.ishkur.com/). It's not unlike speed-running the bins of a vast record warehouse—nerdier than spending an afternoon at Berlin's Bikini Waxx, but still digging at heart. cosine, meanwhile, uses spectral images of inputted sound based on tone and composition to generate recommendations. In simple language, if you like a thing, it can present you with more of that thing at a strictly sonic level, innoculated from the influence of PR, playlists or muddied genre tags.  
  
When they hit, tools like these can spark a leap in knowledge or reorientation of personal taste, just as quirky sites dedicated to [globetrotting](https://radio.garden/) and [time-travelling](https://app.radiooooo.com/) radio once did. But the harsh reality is that the industry has no reason to finance them since they loosen the dominant grip corporations work hard to maintain. When has something ever been propped up just for the sake of public good?

So, if you're a true completist, you may end up getting into piracy instead.  
  
Be it Soulseek, What.cd, Oink or lock-and-key forums, many of the rigorous open-eared listeners (and touring DJs) I know don't pay for music downloads. At all. There's something to be said for the fact that the most eager music communities are usually adjacent to piracy, a quirk of industry retaliation and obsession that we'll save for another time. Here's the irony: corporations might one day rely on their graces should we tip into a digital dark age.  
  
Save for trillions of pages mercifully saved by the Wayback Machine, pirate systems remain perhaps the most stable repository for niche culture. With multiple steps to ratify entry and the need to maintain a tight upload/download ratio, they're a walled garden requiring an investment of time and attention. As such, they're unlikely to move the needle for Zoomers for whom even a digital webstore or Safari tab feels old hat. Yet when What.cd went down in 2016, it took with it one of the purest music discovery environments the internet has produced. It was a potent case study for the music industry's untapped potential, should it serve a different, purer set of interests.  
  
"Don't forget," What.cd's departing staff [wrote](https://x.com/whatcd/status/799751019294965760/) ten years ago, "there are people who are willing to work to ensure that works of cultural value can be preserved and shared." Data and information is a volatile currency, but if it goes to zero, we all lose.

## Chapter III The Underground Needs A Realistic Framework for AI

Which leads us to the great scrambler of AI. Except, this might not go how you expect. It's not about the slop washing up ashore, nor the low cloud of auto-generated summaries blanketing the internet. The impact of AI on production standards, taste and the very experience of interacting with electronic music is set to whip through life like a Catherine wheel. Given where we are, confronting the ramifications of that seems like the larger issue to tackle.  
  
I'll preface this by saying that I have zero interest in seeking out or listening to AI music over human-made sound—a disclaimer that feels infantile to even type—and I don't know a single person who does. Passions are running so hot, this section is probably going to be interpreted as capping for AI no matter what I say or do. And yet, something about the grandstanding that reached fever pitch in response to [Bandcamp's ban](https://ra.co/news/84314) earlier this year struck me as counterintuitive. Maybe even a little forced.  
  
Of course, keeping music human _feels_ like the right message, especially when broadcast by a service who have done so much to drive revenue to independent musicians over the past decade or so. Direct action with a clear endgame, like [protests](https://ra.co/news/85189) pushing for opt-in consent and financial recompense, is the move. When stars like Jack Antonoff [wade in](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/jack-antonoff-ai-music-creators-godless-whores-1235562653/) with overblown posturing, the squall gets louder, reducing a complex issue to a binary response: human good, any level of generative tech bad.  
  
The argument for riding it out until the current bubble bursts is understandable. The fugazi of extreme debt that props up AI's [Big Five](https://www.theatlantic.com/economy/2025/12/nvidia-ai-financing-deals/685197/) is certain to crash out sooner or later, with the hope that it might drag AI music down with it. Yet the way nations are pumping [trillions](http://weforum.org/stories/2026/04/ai-investments-7-trillion-buildout-right/) into infrastructure, and the way even the Pope has [waded in](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/pope/news/2026-05/pope-leo-xiv-encyclical-magnifica-humanitas-ai.html) with a missive even longer than this one, proves we're too far gone for this technology not to reshape professional and public life, including the arts.  
  
Bot-generated music drifting through data centres for the audience of no one does seem profoundly pointless. To musicians who've already winced through studios' worth of DJ or production kit being shrunk down to app size, seeing a GPU replicate their skills may be enough to consider packing it all in. But like every tool since the dawn of industry, it can be wielded in both lazy and useful ways. And if you do believe that cultural anaesthetisation might be the outcome, isn't an aversion to engage naïve?

**A generation desensitised to AI may be unconvinced that independent electronic music, with all its idiosyncrasies and flaws, goes down better**

The fear that follows each advance of technology is nothing new. In 1982, the Music Union, a British trade body that still represents tens of thousands of working musicians, capitulated to skittishness about electronic pop and outlawed the use of synthesisers and drum machines among its members, before walking it back 15 years later. By the 21st century, DAWs had rattled the industry. Using ProTools, one beatmaking legend [claimed](https://www.wired.com/2003/10/producers/) in 2003, was like "cheating"—a rash take given its positive effect on lowering barriers to entry for production, not to mention where said legend, Timbaland, [would end up](https://www.npr.org/2025/06/17/nx-s1-5431544/timbaland-ai-music-suno-stage-zero-tata-generative). The hysteria over AutoTune similarly fizzled in time. We consider Cher's "Believe" a stone-cold banger now, don't we?  
  
Just like the democratising effect of [cracked software](https://ra.co/news/84806), it seems likely that initial uptake will be strongest outside of club culture's power centres. General adoption of AI in Nigeria already hovers north of 85 percent, and polling about societal concern reveals [neither](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/) India (19 percent) nor South Korea (16 percent) are fussed. For now, Western nations with sclerotic economies and powerful creative sectors are nervous, but the glaciers are already [beginning to melt](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/ai-in-music-how-used-now-1235536484/).  
  
"I've seen AI in every studio, in every session," Recording Academy CEO Harvey Mason Jr. recently told _Music Business Worldwide_. "I'm not remembering a song that I've been around or a room that I've been in that was not using some form of AI." If that's the state of play at the corporate level, the drawbridge for independent artists will snap shut, eliminating opportunities from [syncs](https://ra.co/news/84036) to corporate remixes that have long propped up dance music's mid-tier.  
  
So long as the taboo around AI remains a disincentive to use it—or at least _acknowledge_ using it—discerning electronic types will stay on one side of the fence. But getting caught flat-footed carries risks. In 2024, Ableton and Monolake's Robert Henke counseled that incorporation of AI into a production workflow would be inevitable once it became destigmatised, yet it still took Splice two years to poke their head above the parapet with [a way](https://ra.co/news/85011) to fold generative AI into their creator payout model. When French Touch sample wizards Alan Braxe & Fred Falke recently [declared](https://www.musicradar.com/music-tech/i-didnt-get-it-at-first-then-i-visited-my-friends-studio-the-way-he-was-using-it-opened-my-eyes-house-icons-alan-braxe-and-fred-falke-on-embracing-ai-in-the-studio) their appreciation of AI within their creative process a few months back, it still felt like an outlier.  
  
Fundamentally, whether cautiously pro- or vehemently anti-AI, institutional preparedness is a safe bet. As the bargaining power of human-made art is being weakened by the advance of AI, defence needs to be crafty. Because while some deals train up systems and others try to jam them in the courts, the technology simply grows stronger and its market share larger. And on all fronts, Suno is lapping the competition.

Suno isn't only the most advanced product out there, but secure enough to now be in an [acquisitive mood](https://ra.co/news/85117). Unlike OpenAI's much-hyped yet now-shuttered Sora, which was both a [legal liability](https://www.wheresyoured.at/sora2-openai/#sora-2-is-unbelievably-expensive-with-each-video-costing-5-of-compute-and-users-allowed-to-generate-100-videos-every-24-hours-%E2%80%94-meaning-openai-will-lose-a-month%E2%80%99s-subscription-revenue-every-four-videos-generated) and swiftly abandoned by users, Suno's base is highly active. They generate the equivalent of Spotify's _entire_ 100 million catalogue size every two weeks, before uploading heaps of it onto streaming services to slosh around in perpetuity.  
  
The Suno subreddit, whose users dwarf communities dedicated to Berghain or propertechno many times over, is like stepping into another reality. [Prompt lists](https://www.reddit.com/r/SunoAI/comments/1mkknun/get_the_vocals_you_want/#lightbox) expose just how replicable electronic production has become. For those still invested in chillwave, fiddle around with shimmer reverb and stereo widening to nail the vocals; as for trip-hop's "smoky contralto," it can be boiled down to ribbon mic darkness rather than the actual disquiet that gave Beth Gibbons and Martina Topley-Bird their tone.  
  
These facsimiles are essentially 95 percent of the way there, and while we might argue that the final five percent is what elevates good compositions into great music, it's a losing pitch for a community who already see the value of human-made art as nil. "Give the world another one-to-two years," reads one telling comment, "and nobody cares anymore if anything is AI or not."  
  
It sounds like an extreme reach. To test it, I committed an entire afternoon's listening to AI music and nothing else. My guide was a South Korean-located, 100 percent GPU-generated channel by the name of [what is ?](https://www.youtube.com/@whatisblank) The tunes were pleasantly enough, with jazzy numbers coming off like [Chick Corea](https://music.apple.com/album/light-as-a-feather/1443203400) and slick beats that could pass for [Metal Fingers](https://mfdoom.bandcamp.com/album/metal-fingers-presents-special-herbs-the-box-set-vol-0-9), even if none of the songs lasted longer than a couple minutes. Honestly, though? It suited writing better than what I'd had on prior, a set of gamelan recordings that could clang disruptively, and Autechre's _Tri Repetae_, which naturally asks more of listeners.  
  
After a while I tapped out, mostly due to boredom, but to claim that my soul yearned for something _real_ would be as artificial as the uploads themselves. While ingesting too much slop in one go is liable to leave you queasy, a generation desensitised to AI may be unconvinced that independently made electronic music, with all its idiosyncrasies and flaws, goes down better.

**"Why wouldn't humans thirsty for new sounds use models that let you create sounds in new and strange ways?" – Holly Herndon**

The industry has long been rigged to foist passivity onto us, using all manner of algorithmic and behavioural tactics, as [Liz Pelly](https://ra.co/exchange/819) chronicled in last year's excellent book, _Mood Machine_. Yet away from nefarious corporate influence, there's a gigantic and comparatively unheralded band of listeners who actively relish music in its most passive form.  
  
Of all the depersonalised mood playlists, none have had the impact or staying power of the beat loops you can find on [Lofi Girl](https://www.youtube.com/@LofiGirl/videos). The always-on YouTube channel, best known for "lo-fi hip-hop radio 📚 beats to relax/study to," receives short shrift from the heads: mere "Starbucks music," [per](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/feb/19/musician-and-film-maker-flying-lotus-the-whole-lo-fi-beats-thing-has-become-like-starbucks-music) Flying Lotus. Yet since 2015, it's drawn an eye-watering 2.59 billion cumulative plays, at time of press, making it statistically among the most popular electronic music on earth.  
  
Over the past decade, city pop has also surged into an unlikely monolith.. A resurgence that began around the time of vaporwave was then sustained through mid-'10s chill culture, with city pop's pliant grooves and nonspecific nostalgia a perfect match for Pacific Rhythm shirts and Steely Dan hats. The popularity of the _Pacific Breeze_ compilation was a boon for Light In The Attic, while the demand for Hiroshi Nagai screen prints must have been insane—just as the posthumous heralding of Nujabes as godfather of lo-fi has pushed many of his songs into the [eight-figure range](http://open.spotify.com/album/6nVACH6a27eOWiumAJhDWS).  
  
But the exact qualities that appeal to casual listeners are proving its undoing. [City](https://www.youtube.com/@ChillCityPop/posts) [slop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3E9NNRo1op8) is on the rise, an infinity pool of "[Plastic Love](https://open.spotify.com/track/7rU6Iebxzlvqy5t857bKFq)" knockoffs with vaguely '80s-coded visuals and lyrics that, for the most part, raise no alarm since the majority of consumers don't speak the language in the first place. Purists are irate; lo-fiers have now notably taken to tagging "NO AI 🚫" in their "Study, Work & Chill 🤍" playlists.  
  
All this might raise a cynical smirk: mood music influencing the conditions of its production to such a degree that an even more streamlined competitor emerges to threaten it is, in many ways, a natural development in the saga. But take a beat. Hone in on that 17-year-old who stands a chance of worming their way into the underground. It's bad enough that the media ignores this enormous band of the listener pyramid. Wouldn't it make sense to focus on converting listeners with an affinity for electronic beats into active fans? Do we want to abandon them to a Suno loop for the rest of time? And if AI is now mimicking any imitable sound, what's to stop DJ mixes and club music from being next?

## Chapter IV The Road Less Sentimental

When the gulf between a greying underground stressing the ethicality of generative tools and a generation blowing their ears out to [phonk edits](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxtx5lgqMSc) of Tung Tung Tung Sahur is widening, the smart move for electronic communities is to opt for pragmatism, even if it's devoid of romance.  
  
In theory, club culture becoming the final refuge from AI music could provide a major shot in the arm, but it would require sector-wide rejection. Even if musicians decide it's a red line, would ravers recognise generic 4/4 pump completed with AI samples? Will touring DJs take on extra work to filter out assisted promos as a matter of principle? The minimal uptake in [Aslice](https://ra.co/news/81207) suggests otherwise.  
  
Besides, dance enthusiasts' vigilance is already getting stress-tested. Fans of Shanti Celeste may have enjoyed a pair of tracks released across [various](https://music.amazon.co.uk/albums/B0GLXRBBFN) [streaming](https://www.deezer.com/en/album/913206441) [services](https://tidal.com/album/496409925) this February, featuring Peach Discs's signature bubbly artwork and flair. Just one problem: they're all fakes. And still up months later.

**Better to extract value from it before it extracts value from you**

And so, AI creep is liable to continue unchecked. When the first scandal over a notable producer covertly using AI inevitably hits, sure, it might mirror the ghostwriting storms in the mid-'10s for a day or two. But what of labels already consulting Gemini for market research or designers feeding prompts into Midjourney? Do they constitute substantial usage? Early blowback surrounding visual slop by major clubs on social media has faded, to the point where DJs are casually [tossing it](https://www.instagram.com/reels/DSVeLpcDDU0/) around on the grid, and the telltale signs of AI release copy are everywhere. Truth nuke: any hard boundary separating the two realms, at this point, is porous and largely imaginary.  
  
When consulted for a recent edition of [_RA Pro_](https://ra.co/news/84528) on the topic, Holly Herndon cut to the heart of how the underground's ingrained resistance to change on this topic may boomerang: "I'm compelled to push back against banning human artists for experimenting with an era-defining medium. A lazy assumption with AI is that the laziest people use it, and the most dedicated people use traditional tools."  
  
She continued: "This is sentimentalism. Many of the producers I know to be the most adventurous are fascinated with what models can enable for them…Why wouldn't humans thirsty for new sounds use models that let you create sounds in new and strange ways?"  
  
When that day comes, the Overton window will shift and the baseline for complexity will rise. Jay Caspian Kang, one of the _New Yorker_'s sharpest voices on the topic, noted in a [column](https://www.newyorker.com/news/fault-lines/sora-2-and-the-limits-of-digital-narcissism) last year that "a computer first beat a chess grandmaster in 1989, [yet] chess has never been more popular than it is today." If the fear factor is overcooked, and echo chambers act as limiters, why avoid it? Who's to say that the late-'20s successor to Steve Reich running 18 Suno interpretations of the same motif won't produce the sweetest sound humanity has ever heard?  
  
My optimistic hunch is that we'll look back on "[Godmother](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc9OjL6Mjqo)," Herndon's 2019 collaboration with Jlin and her own generated Spawn, the way we do Silver Apples or Delia Derbyshire: a rough draft of a future that wouldn't fully bloom for another decade. It won't be long until the slop wave recedes and mid-'20s aesthetics will spark fascination in the same way Winamp and Napster do today. Some crafty label is probably already A&Ring the sound of early AI music, a compilation that'll surely draw a nice write-up on _RA_ in due course. Today's cultural detritus is tomorrow's nostalgia. We may even feel a kind of fondness for it.

Until then, the future lies bleeding out, the past is warping and the present can feel as if it's slipping through our fingertips. Educational outlets like [CDR](https://www.createdefinerelease.com/), [UMA](https://www.undergroundmusicacademy.com/) and [3024](https://martyn3024.com/mentoring3024/) are doing admirable work to keep artists on the straight path with mentorship and workshops, but their limited scope means good energy can only travel so far. Besides, placing the pressure on participants in culture to assume a twin role of creation and preservation is a tall ask, given how cash- and time-poor just about everybody is.  
  
In the face of sweeping change, we as individuals can feel totally overmatched. Collective action might trigger greater awareness for the internet's systematic decline, but it's tricky to know what solutions would stem the flow of loss.  
  
Pressure campaigns? Open-source repositories? An endowment fund to centralise all culture reporting, music journalism, user uploads, social media and forum threads? There are few instances where modern sports gets it right, but football's pyramid of redistribution that funds academies, invests in youth-level development and mandates downward compensation is a potential model worth following. Even a modest investment by some of the world's largest record companies might build an ecosystem that could work as a digital Library of Alexandria. An industry sweating bullets about young people's resistance would do worse than starting there.  
  
If lineage is as crucial to underground music as we believe it to be, then the intergenerational bridges we rely on need to be more dynamic, flexible and resilient. There's no guarantee the messages will carry through 12-inches or sound systems any more. They're going to live and die online.  
  
If we can allocate resources to safeguard the past while rousing ourselves to embrace the future, no matter how farcical it might feel at this moment in time, culture will benefit. Technopessism is merited, but too easy; try and claw your way to technorealism. And even if you have zero positivity whatsoever, what better way to thumb your nose at tech barons than by flipping their tools against them to strengthen the hand of real art? Better to extract value from it before it extracts value from you.
